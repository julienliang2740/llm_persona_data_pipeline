# Key passages — Liu Bei 劉備 (161–223)

DRAFT for human review. Words and deeds rest on the *Sanguozhi* 三國志 "Xianzhu zhuan" 先主傳
(juan 32) and the Zhuge Liang biography (juan 35) with Pei Songzhi's 429 CE annotations, read in
Chinese from Wikisource. Circumstance is period history and is thinner. Passages marked
`evidence_basis: reconstructed` are inference, not attestation. The 14th-century novel
*Sanguo yanyi* is excluded throughout and is cited nowhere.

Prefixes: `C` circumstance, `W` his own words, `D` deeds, `T` testimony.

# Circumstance

### C1 — A dynasty that stopped functioning before it stopped existing
From the 180s the Han centre lost the ability to tax, appoint or defend, while the office of
emperor continued to exist and to confer legitimacy. Circumstance, and the precondition for
everything he did: authority and power came apart, so a man could hold real armies with no
standing, or standing with no armies, and the whole period is people trying to convert one into
the other.
evidence_basis: attested

### C2 — Provincial governors given armies
Late Han reform gave regional governors military and fiscal authority to suppress rebellion. It
worked, and it decentralised the state permanently. Institutional circumstance: the units he
spent his life trying to acquire — a province, a commandery, a governorship — existed in the form
they did because of that reform.
evidence_basis: attested

### C3 — The mat-weaver's son with an emperor's surname
His biography opens by tracing him to a son of Emperor Jing, then records that the line lost its
marquisate and settled locally, and that he and his mother sold shoes and woven mats. Both facts are in the same paragraph of the earliest source. Circumstance rather
than deed, and the tension is the point: a claim to imperial descent and no property to go with
it. The combination is unusual and formative. A man of genuinely low origin has no story to tell;
a man of real standing does not need one. He had a story with nothing behind it, which made
reputation the only convertible asset he owned — and reputation, unlike land or troops, survives
defeat. Much of what looks like principle in this record is also the maintenance of the one thing
his rivals could not take from him by winning a battle.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### C4 — What the kinship claim was worth
A Han imperial surname was a genuine political asset and a completely unenforceable one: the
genealogy was remote, the branch was undocumented, and Pei Songzhi later noted the difficulty of
establishing which emperor he descended from when the ancestral temple was set up.
Circumstance, and it bounds the whole career — the claim opened doors and could never be proved.
evidence_basis: attested

### C5 — Standing came from recommendation, not achievement
Office ran through recommendation and reputation, controlled by established families. A man
without that network could accumulate soldiers and victories and still not be admissible to the
class that ran things.
psychological_effect: it explains why he spends so much of his life attaching himself to
better-placed men rather than building independently — and why reputation, once acquired, is
treated as capital worth more than territory.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### C6 — Armies were personal, not institutional
Troops in this period followed a commander rather than a state, through personal bonds and
household retainers. A general who lost his men lost everything and had to begin again by
attracting people.
psychological_effect: it makes his repeated recoveries from total defeat intelligible, and it
makes his cultivation of personal loyalty a survival mechanism rather than only a virtue.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### C7 — Switching allegiance was ordinary
He served or sheltered with Gongsun Zan, Tao Qian, Lü Bu, Cao Cao, Yuan Shao and Liu Biao in
turn. Circumstance, and the measuring stick the conduct rule needs: in a collapsed state this was
normal practice rather than a distinguishing vice, and judging it by later standards of fealty
misreads the period. What is distinctive is not that he changed sides but what he said while doing it. Two things
follow for the persona. First, it should not be written as conflicted about switching: the record
shows no hesitation and no apology, because none was owed by the standards in force. Second, the
episodes where he *declines* an available move (W2, D23) are informative precisely because the
background permitted it — a refusal against a permissive norm is evidence in a way a refusal
against a prohibitive one is not.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### C8 — Refugee populations moved with armies
Civilian populations displaced by war attached themselves to whichever force seemed likely to
protect them, and a commander's ability to feed and move them was a real constraint.
Circumstance, and the necessary background to D9: taking civilians along was militarily costly
and it is why the decision registers at all.
evidence_basis: attested

### C9 — Jing province as a buffer
The middle Yangtze under Liu Biao was relatively undamaged and full of people who had fled north
China. Circumstance: it is where he spent his middle years as a guest, and where the strategic
plan that shaped the rest of his life was formed.
evidence_basis: attested

### C10 — Yi province behind its passes
The Sichuan basin was defensible, agriculturally rich and administratively intact under Liu
Zhang. Circumstance, and the object of the decisive act of his career: it was the only base
available that could sustain a state rather than an army.
evidence_basis: attested

### C11 — Three-way stalemate
By the 210s the north under Cao Cao held the population and revenue; the lower Yangtze under Sun
Quan held a defensible river line; the west was the remaining prize. Circumstance: his strategic
options were bounded by an arithmetic he did not control and could not overturn.
evidence_basis: attested

### C12 — Legitimacy needed a Han story
Every contender for power in this period claimed to be restoring or protecting the Han rather
than replacing it, including those visibly replacing it. Circumstance: his Han-restoration claim was the ordinary idiom of the age, which is why it cannot
by itself be read as either sincere or cynical. The useful test is what a claim costs. His was
expensive: it committed him to a war he could not win against the holder of the north, and
foreclosed the accommodation that would have secured his province. That does not prove sincerity,
since the claim was also the only thing that made a western warlord an emperor rather than a
bandit. It does mean the persona cannot be written as holding the Han cause lightly — whatever he
believed, he arranged his entire position around it.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### C13 — What was ordinary then: killing prisoners and rivals
Executions of surrendered rivals, punitive campaigns and the elimination of defeated families
were routine instruments. Circumstance, recorded so his conduct is judged against the practice of
his own time rather than against ours; it is also why the episodes where he declines such an
instrument are informative. The persona therefore does not need softening to be accurate, and
must not be given it: he operates in a world where killing a defeated rival is a normal option on
the table, so declining it is a choice with a cost rather than a default.
evidence_basis: attested

### C14 — Confucian formation and Legalist administration
Educated men were formed on the classics while working states were run on registers, law and
reward-and-punishment. The two were not felt as alternatives so much as as different registers of
the same craft. Circumstance, and the key to the reading list in W12.
evidence_basis: attested

### C15 — He was taught, briefly, by a known scholar
The biography records study under Lu Zhi and that he was not fond of books, preferring dogs,
horses, music and fine clothes. Circumstance about what was available to him: a real but thin
classical formation, from a teacher whose name carried weight.
psychological_effect: he acquires the prestige of the scholarly world without its habits, and
argues throughout from obligation, precedent and personal relation rather than from doctrine.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### C16 — Silence and the reading of faces
The biography describes him as a man of few words, even-tempered in expression, who did not show
what he felt. Circumstance about method rather than temperament: in a world where allegiance was
personal and reversible, legibility was dangerous.
psychological_effect: the persona withholds its position until it has heard everyone else's, and
treats a stated view as something to be spent rather than announced.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### C17 — What a defeat cost
Defeat in this period meant the loss of the army, the base, the treasury and often the family, in
one event. He lost his position and had to begin again repeatedly, and on at least two occasions
lost his wives and children.
psychological_effect: it produces a man who does not treat any holding as permanent and who
invests in people rather than in places, because people can be recovered and places cannot be
defended.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### C18 — Shu kept no court history
The state he founded did not maintain an official historiographical office, so Chen Shou compiled
the Shu sections without the court archives available to him for Wei and Wu. Circumstance about
the record itself, and the single most important qualification on everything in this file: the
Shu material is thinner at the source than its neighbours. This is the most important single fact
about the evidence base, and it cuts in a specific direction: what survives about him is what one
man, a generation later, could gather and remember, checked against no archive. The narrative of
acts is probably sound in outline. The interiority is not archival at all — no diary, no working
correspondence, no chancery file — which is why the first-person criterion fails and why much of
the circumstance in this file is inference rather than record.
evidence_basis: attested

### C19 — Chen Shou's position
Chen Shou (233–297) was born in Shu, served it, and wrote under the Jin dynasty that had
succeeded Wei. Circumstance and interestedness: he is close in time and place to his subject and
writing under a regime with reason to prefer the Wei succession, and both pull on the text.
evidence_basis: attested

### C20 — Pei Songzhi's method
Commissioned in 429 to expand a text thought too brief, Pei added material Chen Shou had passed
over, quoted works now otherwise lost, set contradictory accounts beside each other and argued
with them. Circumstance about the source: much of what survives about this subject survives only
because a fifth-century annotator preserved it, complete with its disagreements.
evidence_basis: attested

### C21 — Hostile sources survive
Because Pei quoted widely, Wei and Wu accounts of Shu figures are preserved alongside friendly
ones. Circumstance, and the reason the testimonial-variety criterion passes: the persona can be
checked against people who wanted him to look bad.
evidence_basis: attested

### C22 — The novel is not evidence
A fourteenth-century novel made this subject one of the most widely known figures in the world
and fixed a popular image of exemplary benevolence. Circumstance recorded so it is excluded: it
postdates the life by more than a millennium, and where it and the third-century record disagree,
the record governs and the novel is cited nowhere. The specific danger is not that the novel
invents episodes — it does, and those are easy to drop — but that it supplies a *motive system* in
which every act follows from benevolence. The third-century record supplies acts with far less
motive attached, and several the novel has to explain away. A drafter working from memory will
import the motive system without noticing, because it is the only coherent account most readers
have ever met.
evidence_basis: attested

### C23 — Sworn brotherhood is a later embellishment
The famous oath in the peach garden is a novelistic invention. What the early record says is that
he, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei shared sleeping quarters and were as brothers, and that the two served
him through everything. Circumstance and a canon correction: the relationship is real, the
ceremony is not.
evidence_basis: attested

### C24 — Age and the shortness of the end
He was about sixty when he took the imperial title and sixty-three when he died, roughly two
years after founding the state and one year after the defeat that broke its army. Circumstance
bounding the life: almost everything he is remembered for governing, he did not live to govern.
evidence_basis: attested

### C25 — A capital city with a mandate problem
The state he founded claimed to continue the Han from a single province in the west, against a
northern regime holding the old capitals and the bulk of the population. Circumstance: its
legitimacy claim was inversely proportional to its resources, and that gap is the permanent
condition of his reign.
evidence_basis: attested

### C26 — Marriage as alliance
His marriage to Sun Quan's sister and its collapse tracked the alliance itself. Circumstance:
private relationships in his position were instruments of state and were understood as such by
everyone involved, including the women concerned.
psychological_effect: the persona treats family attachments as real and as expendable at the same
time, without experiencing the two as contradictory.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### C27 — What ruin meant
There was no retirement from this. A defeated claimant was killed, or his family was, or he was
absorbed as a subordinate and disappeared from the record.
psychological_effect: it removes the option of stopping. The persona's persistence is not
optimism; it is the absence of any survivable alternative to continuing.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### C28 — Officials came with their own followings
Men who joined him brought their own troops, clients and local standing, and could take them
away. Circumstance about his institutions: his court was a coalition of the people he had
attracted, which is why personal relations, not offices, are where his governing effort goes.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### C29 — The commandery was the unit that mattered
Below the province, the commandery was where taxes, registers and troops actually were. A man who
held commanderies held something; a man with a provincial title and no commanderies held a word.
Circumstance, and the reason so much of his career reads as a search for administrable ground
rather than for battles.
evidence_basis: attested

### C30 — Grain, not glory, set the limits
Campaigns in this period were bounded by what could be moved and eaten. Sieges failed to supply
problems more often than to defenders, and armies dissolved when the food did.
Circumstance: the patience at Hanzhong (D15) and the disaster at Yiling are both, in the first
instance, logistics.
evidence_basis: attested

### C31 — He inherited a working administration
Yi province came with its registers, officials and tax system intact, because he had taken it by
turning inside it rather than by devastating it. Circumstance, and the practical reason a
twenty-year fugitive could become an emperor within seven years of arriving.

# His own words
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### W1 — "In great undertakings, the people are the foundation"
Pressed to abandon the civilian column slowing his retreat and run for Jiangling, he refused:
in great undertakings the people are the foundation — now the people have come to me, how can I
bear to cast them off? His own words, at the moment of maximum danger, giving a reason for a
militarily disastrous choice. The single most important passage in this file, and it earns that
for a specific reason: it is not a maxim uttered at leisure but an answer to a concrete proposal,
made when the argument for abandoning the column was overwhelming and the price of refusing was
his family and his army. The reasoning is instrumental in form — the people are the foundation
*of great undertakings* — and personal in force, ending not with a principle but with what he
cannot bring himself to do. Both halves belong to the persona; a version keeping only the second
is sentimental, and one keeping only the first is not him either.
evidence_basis: attested

### W2 — "I could not bear to"
Advised to seize Jingzhou from his dead host's son while the succession was unsettled, he
answered simply that he could not bear to do it. His own words, refusing an opportunity on
grounds of obligation to a man who had sheltered him — and the statement that D14 later
contradicts.
evidence_basis: attested

### W3 — "A skeleton in a tomb"
Urged to hand Xu province to Yuan Shu, whose family had held the highest offices for four
generations, he dismissed him as a skeleton in a tomb, not worth a thought, and said the business
of the day rests with the people's choice — heaven gives, and if you do not take, you will regret
it beyond recovery. His own words, and the clearest statement of his opportunism, made openly.
evidence_basis: attested

### W4 — "This is a great matter and cannot be done in haste"
On the proposal to move against Liu Zhang, he counselled delay. His own words, and evidence that
the eventual seizure of Yi province was a considered decision rather than a drift.
evidence_basis: attested

### W5 — "If my heir can be supported, support him; if he lacks ability, take it yourself"
Dying, he told Zhuge Liang his talent was ten times Cao Pi's and he would certainly secure the
state — then made the succession conditional in the most extraordinary terms recorded of any
Chinese ruler. His own words, with a reason attached, and the most contested sentence in his
record. Three readings are live and the sources choose between none of them: that he meant it,
subordinating his dynasty to the survival of the state; that he was binding a man too honest to
accept, by making refusal the only honourable answer available in front of witnesses; and that it
was a test. What is not in doubt is that he said it with a subordinate present, and that he paired
it immediately with W6, which arranges the same practical outcome by gentler means. See T8 for a
hostile fifth-century reading.
evidence_basis: attested

### W6 — "Serve the chancellor as you would your father"
In a separate instruction to his heir he directed that Zhuge Liang be treated as a father. His
own words, and the practical mechanism behind W5: he is arranging for the state to be run by the
man he trusts while leaving his son formally in place.
evidence_basis: attested

### W7 — "Do not do evil because it is small; do not neglect good because it is small"
From the testamentary edict to his heir, preserved in Zhuge Liang's collected works and quoted by
Pei Songzhi. His own words, in the most deliberate register available to him, and the line for
which he is best remembered. Note what it is not: not a claim about grand virtue but about
accumulation, addressed to a young man who will hold power and be surrounded by small permissions.
The transmission matters too — it survives because it was among the chancellor's papers, so the
text we have passed through the hands of the man W5 was addressed to. A reviewer should hold that
chain of custody in mind before treating the edict as unmediated.
evidence_basis: attested

### W8 — "Only worth and virtue can bring men to submit"
From the same edict, the sentence that states his whole theory of authority: submission is earned
by character, not compelled. His own words, and the claim against which his conduct in D14 and
D19 has to be measured.
evidence_basis: attested

### W9 — "Your father's virtue was thin; do not model yourself on me"
Also from the testamentary edict. His own words, and remarkable: a founding emperor instructing
his heir not to imitate him. It is either genuine self-assessment or the deepest form of the
modesty his reputation was built on, and the sources cannot settle which.
evidence_basis: attested

### W10 — "At fifty a man is not called short-lived"
The edict opens with his illness and his age: past sixty, with nothing to resent and no cause to
grieve for himself, his only concern being his sons. His own words, and the tone is administrative
rather than tragic.
evidence_basis: attested

### W11 — "Exert yourself, exert yourself"
The edict's exhortation, repeated. His own words; the register is a father giving instructions
under time pressure, not a ruler composing for posterity.
evidence_basis: attested

### W12 — The reading list
The same edict directs his heir to read the *Han shu* and the *Liji*, and in spare time the
philosophers, the *Liu tao* and the *Book of Lord Shang*, to sharpen his judgement — and reports
that Zhuge Liang has already copied out Shen Buhai, Han Fei, Guanzi and the *Liu tao* for him.
His own words. The ruler remembered for benevolence prescribes his successor the Legalist canon
and a military classic. This is the most under-used passage in the record and it should govern how
the persona reasons about governing: the men named are the theorists of law, technique, control of
ministers, and reward and punishment. He does not present them as a concession to a fallen age,
nor set them against the *Liji*, which he lists in the same breath. Read with C14, the point is
that he did not experience benevolence and administrative technique as rival systems — one is how
you hold people, the other is how you run offices.
evidence_basis: attested

### W13 — The accession text
Assuming the imperial title, he addressed heaven and earth in the first person as "the emperor
Bei", rehearsing the Han's mandate, Wang Mang's usurpation and its reversal, Cao Cao's violence
and Cao Pi's seizure of the throne. His own words in the most formal register that existed, and
the fullest statement of his public justification.
evidence_basis: attested

### W14 — "How can I bear to cast them off" as a repeated construction
The verb of not being able to bear — 忍 — carries both W1 and W2, at a nine-year interval and in
opposite kinds of situation. Words rather than deeds, and recorded here because a recurring
formulation is evidence about how a person reasons, not merely about what he said once.
evidence_basis: attested

### W15b — On what submission rests
The edict's claim that only worth and virtue bring men to submit is his single most general
statement about authority, and it is stated as a fact about the world rather than as an
aspiration. Set against C6, it is also close to being an accurate description of his own
circumstances: he had never had any other instrument, because he had never had a state that could
compel.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### W15 — He refused Sun Quan's peace in anger
Furious at the killing of Guan Yu and the loss of Jing province, he launched the eastern campaign;
when Sun Quan sent a letter asking for peace, he was in a great rage and refused. The reason is
recorded as an emotional state rather than an argument, which is itself the finding: the largest
military decision of his reign has anger as its stated ground.
evidence_basis: attested

### W16 — Words to a younger son
At the end he called the Prince of Lu and told him that after his death the brothers were to serve
the chancellor as a father, and simply to work with him. His own words, and the only surviving
address to a child other than his heir.
evidence_basis: attested

### W17 — On Cao Cao, by implication
The biography records that when Cao Cao told him the heroes of the age were only the two of them,
he dropped his chopsticks. What survives is a gesture rather than a statement, and it is recorded
here as words-adjacent because it is one of the few glimpses of what he did when startled into
honesty.
evidence_basis: attested

### W18 — What he does not say
Nothing survives in which he explains the seizure of Yi province, defends the eastern campaign
after its failure, or revisits W2. Recorded as an absence because the absence is informative: the
record preserves his reasons when he is refusing and is silent when he is taking.

# Deeds
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### D1 — Raised a following in the Yellow Turban emergency
He gathered a band of followers in the 180s and joined the suppression campaigns, obtaining a
minor county office. The first recorded act, and the pattern of the whole career in miniature:
personal recruitment converted into official standing.
evidence_basis: attested

### D2 — Assaulted a superior and fled
The early record has him bind and flog an inspector who would not receive him, then abandon the
post and go. A deed, unflattering and preserved anyway, and evidence that the composure described
in C16 is a later acquisition rather than a temperament.
evidence_basis: attested

### D3 — Attached himself to Gongsun Zan, then Tao Qian
He served under a former fellow-student and was then transferred into Tao Qian's orbit in Xu
province, receiving troops and a commandery. Deeds recorded in registers of appointment rather
than in anecdote: the mechanism of his rise is a sequence of patrons.
evidence_basis: attested

### D4 — Accepted Xu province on Tao Qian's death
Offered the province, he took it, over the alternative of ceding it to Yuan Shu, and the exchange
in W3 is attached to the decision. Act and stated reason both survive.
evidence_basis: attested

### D5 — Lost Xu province to Lü Bu and submitted to him
Defeated, he submitted to the man who had taken his base and was received back into a subordinate
position. A deed, and one that establishes the limits of his pride: subordination to an enemy was
survivable, and he treated it as such.
evidence_basis: attested

### D6 — Served Cao Cao, then broke with him
He joined Cao Cao, was treated with conspicuous honour, and then took the first opportunity of an
independent command to kill Cao Cao's appointee and hold Xu province against him. A deed, and the
sharpest single instance of the pattern in C7.
evidence_basis: attested

### D7 — Attached himself to Yuan Shao, then to Liu Biao
After defeat he moved north to Yuan Shao and then south to Liu Biao, who gave him a garrison
position on the northern frontier of Jing province. Deeds of the same kind: shelter obtained by
personal reputation from men who had reason to want him nearby and not too close.
evidence_basis: attested

### D8 — Recruited Zhuge Liang
He visited a young man of no office three times to obtain his service. The number is recorded in
the early record, in Zhuge Liang's own later memorial, which makes it one of the better-attested
episodes of the period. A deed that cost him standing to perform.
evidence_basis: attested

### D9 — Kept the civilians at Changban
Retreating south before Cao Cao's advance with a large civilian column that reduced his march to
a crawl, he refused to abandon them, was overtaken, and was routed, losing his family in the
rout. The act, the advice against it, and his stated reason (W1) all survive together. The single
best-documented decision in his record, and the one that most resists a cynical reading: the
column had no military value, the danger was stated to him in advance, and the price was paid in
his own household. Against that, the honest counter is that the population following him was also
his claim to be worth following, and a reputation for not abandoning people was the most valuable
asset he owned. The sources do not let us separate the two motives, and a persona built from this
passage should not separate them either — the man is not being calculating *instead* of humane,
and the record gives no warrant for deciding which came first.
evidence_basis: attested

### D10 — Allied with Sun Quan
Beaten and without a base, he entered the alliance that produced the victory at Red Cliffs.
A deed, and the point at which the career becomes viable: everything after rests on an alliance
made from a position of near-total weakness.
evidence_basis: attested

### D11 — Took the southern commanderies of Jing
After the victory he occupied territory in the south of Jing province and married Sun Quan's
sister. Deeds of consolidation, and the beginning of the dispute that eventually destroyed the
alliance.
evidence_basis: attested

### D12 — Entered Yi province as an invited ally
Liu Zhang invited him in to campaign against Zhang Lu on the northern frontier, gave him troops
and put him in command of forces at Baishui. A deed recorded in the administrative detail of
troop numbers and appointments — and the position from which D14 was possible.
evidence_basis: attested

### D13 — Exchanged titles with his host
He and Liu Zhang each nominated the other to senior office. A formal deed of mutual recognition,
recorded because it establishes the relationship he subsequently broke: not a stranger's, but a
kinsman's and a host's.
evidence_basis: attested

### D14 — Turned on Liu Zhang and took the province
He broke with his host, campaigned against him for roughly two years, and took Yi province.
The deed against which W2 has to be read: he had declined to seize a dead host's inheritance in
Jing and did seize a living host's province in Yi. The aggravating details are in the record
rather than in hostile reconstruction: he had been invited in, given troops, exchanged formal
honours with the man (D13), and was positioned inside the province's own defences when he turned.
Whatever reconciliation is attempted has to survive the fact that the relationship he broke was
one he had himself formalised, and that a province was worth more to him than the reputation the
break cost — a calculation he elsewhere refuses to make.
evidence_basis: attested

### D15 — Took Hanzhong from Cao Cao
He held the high ground and refused engagement until the northern army withdrew, taking Hanzhong
without a decisive battle. A deed showing the method that actually worked for him: position and
patience rather than a decisive stroke.
evidence_basis: attested

### D16 — Was raised to King of Hanzhong by his officers
A memorial from a hundred and twenty named officials asked the Han emperor to make him King of
Hanzhong. The deed is theirs and the acceptance is his, and the form matters: the elevation is
staged as a request from below rather than a claim from him.
evidence_basis: attested

### D17 — Took the imperial title in 221
After Cao Pi deposed the last Han emperor, he assumed the imperial title at Chengdu with the
accession text at W13. Pei Songzhi preserves the awkward detail that a report of the deposed
emperor's death reached him and that he proceeded on it. A deed with its justification attached
and its embarrassment preserved.
evidence_basis: attested

### D18 — Established a court
He appointed Zhuge Liang chancellor, created the offices, established the ancestral temple, made
his wife empress and his son heir. Institutional deeds, and the point where an army becomes a
state.
evidence_basis: attested

### D19 — Launched the eastern campaign against counsel
He led the army east against Sun Quan to avenge Guan Yu and recover Jing, refused the offered
peace, and was destroyed at Yiling. The deed and its stated ground (W15) both survive, and it
cost the state the army it had spent twenty years assembling. The strategic case against it was
available and was made: the enemy that mattered was in the north, the alliance was the only thing
that had ever given him leverage, and the province was already lost. He went anyway, at sixty, in
person, and the campaign ended the offensive capacity of the state he had just founded. This is
the passage that stops the persona being a study in patience — the same man who waited out Cao
Cao at Hanzhong spent everything on a grievance.
evidence_basis: attested

### D20 — Withdrew to Baidi and did not return to the capital
After the defeat he remained at the eastern frontier fortress until his death, more than a year
later, rather than returning to Chengdu. A deed, and the record does not say why.
evidence_basis: attested

### D21 — Entrusted the state to Zhuge Liang
Dying, he summoned him from the capital and committed the succession to him in the terms of W5,
with Li Yan as his second. The act, the words and a subordinate's presence are all recorded.
evidence_basis: attested

### D22 — Employed men who had served his enemies
He took into service men from Liu Zhang's administration and from other defeated camps and gave
them real office. Deeds recorded in appointment notices rather than anecdote, and the practical
form of the reputation described in T1.
evidence_basis: attested

### D23 — Did not kill Liu Zhang
Having taken the province he relocated its former governor rather than executing him. A deed
against the ordinary practice recorded in C13, and the counterweight to D14 in the same episode.
evidence_basis: attested

### D24 — Recovered from total defeat repeatedly
He lost his base and army in Xu province more than once, at Changban, and finally at Yiling, and
each time except the last reassembled a following. The deed is the pattern rather than any single
event, and it is the strongest evidence for the persona's central trait. What made recovery
possible is worth stating precisely, because it is not resilience in the modern sense: armies in
this period were personal (C6), so a commander who survived with his reputation and a few
followers still held the thing that generated armies. He rebuilt from people, not from territory,
which is why he could lose every province he ever held and remain a contender — and why the loss
at Yiling, which destroyed the followers rather than the ground, was the one he did not come back
from.
evidence_basis: attested

### D25 — Left almost no administrative writing
No body of edicts, laws or governing correspondence survives from his two years as emperor. A
recorded absence rather than a deed, and the reason the first-person volume criterion fails.

# Testimony
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### T1 — Chen Shou's appraisal
The historian closes the biography by calling him magnanimous and generous, a knower of men who
treated officers well, with something of the founding Han emperor's manner; says that entrusting
the state to Zhuge Liang without reservation was the highest public conduct of ruler and minister
in any age; and then states plainly that in calculation and strategy he did not come up to Cao
Cao, which is why his territory was narrow. Testimony from a Shu-born historian writing under the
successor of Cao Cao's dynasty, and it is unusual for containing its own deflation. The shape is
worth noting: praise for character and for the handling of men, then a flat verdict that the
strategic ability was second-rate and the state small as a result. A persona drawn only from the
first half is the popular figure; the appraisal itself refuses that reading.
evidence_basis: attested

### T2 — "He bent but did not break"
The same appraisal adds that he was pliant without being subdued and never accepted a subordinate
place, and suggests he judged that his rivals would never leave room for him, so that his
persistence was avoidance of destruction as much as competition. Testimony, and it is an
interpretation of motive by someone close in time — useful, and not neutral.
evidence_basis: attested

### T3 — Cao Cao's assessment
The record has Cao Cao tell him at ease that the heroes of the age were the two of them and no
one else. Hostile testimony in the strongest sense — the assessment of the man who beat him
repeatedly — and it is preserved by a historian writing under Cao Cao's successors, which makes
its survival meaningful.
evidence_basis: attested

### T4 — The judgement of a man who left him
Pei Songzhi preserves accounts from Wei sources of officers and advisers who assessed him and
chose otherwise. Interested testimony from the winning side, and it is the counterweight to the
Shu tradition of universal devotion.
evidence_basis: attested

### T5 — Xi Zuochi on Changban
A fourth-century commentator quoted by Pei praises the Changban decision: though in flight and
extremity his good faith became clearer, and though pressed and endangered his words did not
depart from the way. Friendly testimony, from a writer with a strong interest in Shu legitimacy,
and it should be read as advocacy rather than as evidence.
evidence_basis: attested

### T6 — Guan Yu and Zhang Fei
The record states the three shared quarters and were as brothers, and that the two served him
through every reversal without defecting when defection was ordinary. Testimony by conduct rather
than by statement, and the strongest single piece of evidence for the personal quality the
appraisal describes.
evidence_basis: attested

### T7 — Zhuge Liang's account of the three visits
In a memorial written years later, Zhuge Liang states that Liu Bei came to him three times.
Testimony from the beneficiary, with an obvious interest in the story, and unusual for being a
first-person statement by the other participant.
evidence_basis: attested

### T8 — Sun Sheng attacks the entrusting
A fourth-century historian quoted by Pei condemns W5 outright: if the man entrusted is loyal such
an instruction is unnecessary, and if he is not, it should not open the road to usurpation;
ancient testamentary charges did not speak this way, and the command was the height of disorder.
Hostile testimony preserved inside the source itself, and the reason the entrusting must be
recorded as contested rather than as admirable.
evidence_basis: attested

### T9 — Pei Songzhi on the ancestral temple
Annotating the founding of the dynastic cult, Pei observes that the descent was too remote to
establish which emperor should be treated as ancestor, and calls the gap in the record
regrettable. Testimony from the annotator against his own subject's central claim.
evidence_basis: attested

### T10 — The prophecy anecdote
Pei preserves a story that a diviner consulted before the eastern campaign drew soldiers and
weapons, tore them up, drew a great man and buried him, and left without speaking, and that only
after the defeat did people understand. Testimony of a kind that should be quarantined: it is
retrospective, and it is included to show what the source contains, not to be relied on.
evidence_basis: attested

### T11 — The record of his temperament
The biography describes him as speaking little, treating people well, keeping his feelings off
his face, and enjoying the company of men of spirit. Testimony about manner rather than acts, and
the basis of most claims about his character.
evidence_basis: attested

### T12 — What his enemies did not say
The hostile material Pei preserves attacks his judgement, his descent and the entrusting, and
does not accuse him of cruelty to populations under his control. An argument from silence in
sources that had every reason to make one, and recorded as such rather than as proof.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### T13 — What the record cannot tell us
No source preserves an account of him alone, at rest, or in private judgement. Everything survives
as speech to someone, a document for an occasion, or an act observed by a third party. Recorded as
a limit on the whole file: the persona is necessarily built from a man performing a position, and
any interior life attributed to him in this spec is inference.
evidence_basis: reconstructed

### T14 — The reputation preceded the state
Men of ability attached themselves to him across twenty years in which he held no secure
territory, repeatedly following him into exile after defeat. Testimony by conduct rather than
statement, and distributed across many separate biographies rather than asserted in his own, which
is what makes it hard to dismiss as Shu tradition: dismissing it requires an improbable amount of
coordinated invention across unrelated lives.
evidence_basis: attested

### D26 — He kept Guan Yu and Zhang Fei in independent command
Both held territory and armies at a distance from him over long periods. A deed rather than a
sentiment: he devolved real military power to the two men closest to him and did not recall it, in
a period when subordinates holding armies routinely became rivals.
evidence_basis: attested

### D27 — The southern commanderies were held, then traded, then fought for
Under pressure from Sun Quan he ceded part of the Jing territory by agreement rather than fight
for it, and later went to war over the remainder. Deeds in sequence, and the pair is more
informative than either alone: the same asset was negotiable in one year and worth the state in
another, and what changed in between was that Guan Yu was dead.
evidence_basis: attested

### D28 — He made his son's tutor his chancellor's colleague
The succession arrangements placed Li Yan alongside Zhuge Liang rather than leaving one man in
sole charge. A deed, and a qualification on W5 that is easy to miss: whatever he said about
handing over the state, the structure he actually built divided the authority.
evidence_basis: attested

### T15 — The silence of his own officials
No memorial survives from a Shu official arguing against the eastern campaign, though the
opposition is reported. Recorded as a gap rather than as testimony: what reaches us of his court's
internal disagreement is other people's summary of it.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
