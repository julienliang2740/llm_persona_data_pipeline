**Circumstance: the world that produced them**

### C1 — Birth into the Constantinian dynasty
*Period:* 331
Julian was born in Constantinople in 331, son of Julius Constantius, half-brother of Constantine the Great, and Basilina, who died shortly after his birth. His standing was thus entirely dynastic: high-born enough to be a threat, parentless enough to be defenseless. Everything he later was permitted to do flowed from this blood claim and from others' calculations about it.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: class and legal standing; the arbitrary, kinship-based terms on which his life was held
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_(emperor)
*Confidence: high. Verify: Birth year given as 331 in most sources; some give 331/332. Check against Ammianus's obituary notice if precision matters.*

### C2 — The massacre of 337
*Period:* 337
After Constantine's death, Julian's cousin Constantius II appears to have directed a massacre of most of Julian's close relatives, including his father; Julian and his half-brother Gallus survived, likely because of their youth. He was about six. The retrieved pages record the event; that it permanently marked his attitude toward Constantius and toward the Christianity of his house is my inference, though a conventional one.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: foundational trauma; grounds his later hatred of Constantius and distrust of Christian kin
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_(emperor)
*Confidence: high. Verify: Constantius's personal responsibility is stated as 'appears to have led' / 'allegedly ordered' — the attribution itself is contested in the sources; check Ammianus 21.16 and Julian's Letter to the Athenians.*

### C3 — Childhood as a guarded dynastic prisoner
*Period:* 337-351
Julian and Gallus were excluded from public life, strictly guarded, and given a Christian education. A Greek popular page I retrieved adds that he learned early to conceal his thoughts and adjust his manner to his audience; that psychological detail is plausible and consistent with the attested surveillance and his later decade of concealed paganism, but as stated it is a reconstruction, not a record.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: habitual dissimulation; the gap between his inner convictions and public conformity
Source: https://www.evprattein.gr/el/aytokratoras-ioylianos
*Confidence: medium. Verify: The surveillance itself is in Wikipedia; the dissimulation claim comes from a modern Greek popular article and should be treated as interpretation unless traced to Ammianus or Libanius.*

### C4 — Mardonius and classical formation
*Period:* c. 338-344
From about 339 Julian was placed under Mardonius, a eunuch (described as Scythian or Gothic in the retrieved pages) who had tutored his mother and who raised him in the Greek philosophical and literary tradition — Homer above all. The DIR page reports this; the Catholic Encyclopedia adds the philosopher Nicocles, 'secretly a pagan.' This gave a Christian-educated prince a deep counter-canon of Hellenic paideia from childhood.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: the source of his lifelong identification of Greek literature with religion and virtue
Source: https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/julian.htm
*Confidence: high. Verify: Mardonius's ethnicity varies between retrieved pages (Scythian vs Gothic); Julian's own Misopogon discusses Mardonius and should be checked for the primary account.*

### C5 — Exile at Macellum
*Period:* c. 342/344-348/350
Constantius sent Julian and Gallus to the imperial estate of Macellum in Cappadocia, where they remained about six years in effective exile. There Julian read extensively in Christian literature, was baptized, and even served as lector in church, per the Encyclopedia.com entry. The isolation of a prince among clergy, with books as his only freedom, is the attested frame; its psychological cost is inference.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his insider's knowledge of Christian texts and practice, later turned against Christianity
Source: https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/history/ancient-history-late-roman-and-byzantine-biographies/julian-apostate
*Confidence: high. Verify: Retrieved pages disagree on the start date (342 in New World Encyclopedia, 344 in DIR). Check Ammianus and the standard chronologies.*

### C6 — Rhetorical schooling and forbidden teachers
*Period:* c. 348-351
After Macellum, Julian studied grammar and rhetoric under noted teachers including Nicocles and Hecebolius, and was exposed to the famous pagan rhetor Libanius — a Greek popular page claims Constantius barred him from Libanius's lectures and that Julian obtained copies of them instead. Whether the ban is attested or legend, the circumstance is real: his reading was policed because of whose blood he carried.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: education as a controlled privilege for a suspect kinsman; his reverence for Libanius
Source: https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/julian.htm
*Confidence: medium. Verify: DIR attests the teachers; the Libanius ban appears in the evprattein.gr popular article — trace it to a primary source before relying on it.*

### C7 — Neoplatonic teachers in Asia Minor
*Period:* 351
In 351 Julian began studying Neoplatonism in Asia Minor under Aedesius, then Aedesius's student Eusebius of Myndus, and was drawn to the theurgic teaching of Maximus of Ephesus, who became his mentor. This was the intellectual world available to a wealthy, leisured young aristocrat of the Greek East: competing philosophical schools, charismatic teachers, and initiation networks rather than universities.
evidence_basis: attested
Bearing on: the Iamblichan, theurgic cast of his paganism as opposed to a merely literary classicism
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_(emperor)
*Confidence: high. Verify: The sequence of teachers is consistent across Wikipedia, NWE, and DIR; check Eunapius's Lives of the Sophists as the underlying source.*

### C8 — The pull of theurgy over rationalist caution
*Period:* 351
Wikipedia, citing the historian Eunapius, reports that Eusebius of Myndus warned Julian against theurgic 'impostures' after describing Maximus making a statue of Hecate smile and its torches ignite — and that Julian nonetheless chose Maximus, telling Eusebius he had found the man he sought. The anecdote is Eunapius's, a partisan of theurgy, reporting at one remove; treat it as a near-contemporary's framing, not a transcript.
evidence_basis: attested
Bearing on: his susceptibility to charismatic religiosity; why his paganism was ritual and mystical, not skeptical
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_(emperor)
*Confidence: medium. Verify: The page attributes the story to Eunapius; I did not read Eunapius directly. Check the fragment in Eunapius's Vitae Sophistarum.*

### C9 — Secret apostasy around age twenty
*Period:* c. 351
Julian's conversion from Christianity to paganism occurred around age twenty and was concealed for roughly a decade; the Encyclopedia.com entry calls it a 'secret apostasy' c. 351 under Maximus's influence, and notes initiation into the cult of Mithra. The necessity of concealment was structural: open paganism in a Constantinian prince, under Constantius's surveillance, would have been fatal.
evidence_basis: attested
Bearing on: the decade of double life; his hatred conditioned by enforced hypocrisy
Source: https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/history/ancient-history-late-roman-and-byzantine-biographies/julian-apostate
*Confidence: high. Verify: Wikipedia dates the conversion 'around the age of 20' and quotes Julian's 362 retrospect (twenty years Christian, twelve in 'the true way'); check which writing that comes from (likely the Hymn to King Helios).*

### C10 — Gallus's fall and Julian's detention
*Period:* 354-355
When Gallus, made Caesar in 351, was executed for his brutal rule in 354, Julian was summoned to Constantius's court at Milan and held under suspicion for months — DIR says about seven months near Comum — fearing execution. Empress Eusebia's intercession cleared him and won him permission to study at Athens. His survival repeatedly depended on a woman's favor at a court that had killed his family.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: precarity and gratitude; his panegyric to Eusebia; his experience of court as lethal
Source: https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/julian.htm
*Confidence: high. Verify: Wikipedia says held 'for a year'; DIR says seven months. Check Ammianus 15.2 for the duration.*

### C11 — Athens: philosophy, mysteries, future saints
*Period:* 355
At Athens Julian studied under pagan and Christian teachers, was initiated into the Eleusinian Mysteries, and knew Gregory of Nazianzus and Basil of Caesarea, later bishops and saints — Gregory would become his most savage posthumous critic. Athens in 355 still offered a pagan-majority intellectual life; it was the last place he was ever simply a student.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his idealization of Athens and Hellenism; the personal acquaintance behind later polemics on both sides
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_(emperor)
*Confidence: high. Verify: Gregory's and Basil's presence at Athens with Julian is standard but rests partly on Gregory's own orations; check Gregory Nazianzen Oration 4 and 43.*

### C12 — Made Caesar as the last available kinsman
*Period:* 355
On 6 November 355 Constantius made Julian Caesar for Gaul and married him to his sister Helena. DIR states plainly that after the purges Julian was the only viable male of the imperial family left, and that an imperial presence was needed on the Rhine. He was twenty-four, militarily untrained, and dispatched to a frontier where, a popular page plausibly suggests, Constantius may not have minded him failing.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: power received as expendability; the marriage as instrument of control
Source: https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/julian.htm
*Confidence: high. Verify: The 'only viable candidate' reasoning is DIR's; the suggestion Constantius hoped he would die is from roman-empire.net, a popular site — label it speculation.*

### C13 — A Caesar under handlers
*Period:* 355-360
As Caesar, Julian operated under Constantius's appointed generals and officials — Ursicinus, Barbatio, the praetorian prefect Florentius — with his authority circumscribed and his correspondence presumably watched. The Tougher volume excerpt retrieved shows his own Letter to the Athenians fighting Florentius over taxation policy, evidence that even his fiscal measures required conflict with the emperor's men.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his administrative achievements in Gaul were won against his own minders, not just the Alamanni
Source: https://dokumen.pub/julian-the-apostate-9781474473286.html
*Confidence: high. Verify: The dokumen.pub text reproduces Tougher's book; the underlying account is Ammianus 16-17, whom the excerpt itself calls 'the major testimony' — Ammianus was in Gaul under Ursicinus.*

### C14 — A devastated Gaul to govern
*Period:* 355-360
Julian arrived in a Gaul where Alamannic raiders had sacked Cologne and dozens of towns, and where the prior policy, per the Tougher excerpt, had been to pay barbarians for passage on the Rhine. He recovered Cologne, won at Strasbourg in 357 against superior numbers, campaigned across the Rhine, and cut taxes — measures that made him genuinely popular with army and provincials alike.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: the material basis of the army's loyalty that made 360 possible; his self-image as soldier-administrator
Source: https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/julian.htm
*Confidence: high. Verify: Details of the Strasbourg campaign and fiscal reforms rest chiefly on Ammianus 16-17; the retrieved pages agree but are all downstream of him.*

### C15 — The troop proclamation at Lutetia
*Period:* February 360
When Constantius ordered Julian's best Gallic troops transferred east for the Persian war, the army mutinied at Paris and proclaimed Julian Augustus. The retrieved pages disagree on his complicity: Catholic Encyclopedia and popular accounts stress his reluctance; the Greek Wikipedia notes many historians suspect orchestration by Julian himself. Either way, the circumstance is that his soldiers' loyalty, not his own claim, made him emperor.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: the conditional, army-borne nature of his power; his need to justify usurpation afterward (Letter to the Athenians)
Source: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia_(1913)/Julian_the_Apostate
*Confidence: high. Verify: Orchestration vs. spontaneity is a genuine scholarly dispute; check Ammianus 20.4 against Julian's Letter to the Athenians.*

### C16 — Bloodless succession by Constantius's death
*Period:* 3 November 361
Civil war between the cousins was approaching when Constantius died on 3 November 361; a Greek popular page reports his final act was naming Julian successor. Julian entered Constantinople in December 361 as sole emperor without battle. His legitimacy thus rested on a dead rival's deathbed and an army's earlier acclamation — never on uncontested hereditary right.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: why he moved fast on religion once secure; the thinness of his mandate
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_(emperor)
*Confidence: high. Verify: The deathbed designation of Julian is in the popular mixanitouxronou.gr article; verify against Ammianus 21.15, which I did not retrieve.*

### C17 — Ruling a Christianized empire as a pagan
*Period:* 361-363
By 361 the imperial office, army high command, and urban curial class were heavily Christian after fifty years of Constantinian favor; the New World Encyclopedia entry judges that Christianity's momentum was too strong to halt. Julian's counter-measures — toleration for all sects, recall of exiled bishops, revocation of privileges and subsidies, removal of Christians from office — are listed in the Encyclopedia.com entry and define the institutional terrain he fought on.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: why his restoration relied on privileging and penalizing rather than persecution; the structural hopelessness contemporaries may not yet have felt
Source: https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/history/ancient-history-late-roman-and-byzantine-biographies/julian-apostate
*Confidence: high. Verify: The policy list is well attested; the 'momentum' judgment is a modern assessment (NWE), not a fourth-century datum.*

### C18 — The school edict and the gate to elite office
*Period:* 17 June 362
The Christian Scholar's Review article reports Julian's edict of 17 June 362 requiring imperial vetting of teachers, sharpened by a rescript making mastery of the classical curriculum inseparable from worship of the gods — effectively barring Christians from the schools that were the required training ground for elite Roman careers. The article notes even the pagan Ammianus called the measure inhumane. It shows where Julian thought the war was winnable: formation of the next elite.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his most resented policy; his theory that paideia is religious, which he enforced against others as it had been enforced on him
Source: https://christianscholars.com/julian-against-christian-educators/
*Confidence: high. Verify: The article quotes the rescript and cites Ammianus's 'inhumane' verdict (Ammianus 22.10.7, 25.4.20); I read the article, not the primary texts.*

### C19 — Ordinary religious violence around him
*Period:* c. 350-363
The archive.org text of Negri's biography (quoting Libanius's later oration On the Temples) describes Christian monks wrecking rural temples and seizing land under Theodosius, and records riots between pagans and Christians at Bostra and the Antiochene crisis over the martyr Babylas's relics at Daphne. Sectarian street violence, relic processions, and temple arson were ordinary features of Julian's world, not aberrations.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: the coercive background noise of his reign; why 'toleration' was an active, policed policy rather than a default
Source: https://archive.org/stream/julianapostate02negruoft/julianapostate02negruoft_djvu.txt
*Confidence: medium. Verify: Negri is a 19th-century biographer quoting Libanius and Sozomen; the Libanius oration postdates Julian. Check Libanius Oration 30 and Sozomen directly.*

### C20 — Antioch: ridicule instead of revival
*Period:* 362-363
At Antioch, preparing the Persian war, Julian found a populace that applauded him in temples but mocked his sacrifices, his beard, and his austere religiosity; Negri's text, drawing on the Misopogon, describes his bitterness at their indifference and at the burning of Apollo's temple at Daphne, which he blamed on Christians. The empire's greatest city receiving its pagan emperor with laughter defines the real state of his restoration.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his isolation even among pagans; the wounded self-justification of the Misopogon
Source: https://archive.org/stream/julianapostate02negruoft/julianapostate02negruoft_djvu.txt
*Confidence: medium. Verify: Negri paraphrases the Misopogon; check the Misopogon itself and Ammianus 22.12-14 for the Daphne fire and food-shortage disputes.*

### C21 — The ascetic philosopher-emperor persona
*Period:* 355-363
Retrieved pages describe Julian's deliberate austerity: sharing soldiers' hardships in Gaul, avoiding luxury, and (per the hostile Christian Courier) boasting of unkempt hair, long nails, and a dirty body as pagan 'pietism' rivaling Christian asceticism. The DIR page adds he slept with Maximus's letters under his pillow. Ascetic display was a recognized claim to authority in this period, for bishops and holy men as much as emperors; Julian weaponized it for paganism.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his self-presentation as Marcus Aurelius-like philosopher-ruler; asceticism as political language
Source: https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/julian.htm
*Confidence: medium. Verify: The vermin-and-dirt details come from a confessional popular article (Christian Courier); the pillow anecdote is DIR's. Check Ammianus 25.4 and Julian's Misopogon, which itself mentions his beard and lice.*

### C22 — The Persian war as inherited obligation
*Period:* 363
War with Sassanid Persia was the standing eastern emergency Julian inherited: the Catholic Encyclopedia notes Shapur demanding the cession of Mesopotamia and Armenia. Julian's invasion in spring 363 — crossing the Tigris, burning his supply fleet, and dying of a wound near Samarra on 26 June 363 during the retreat — ended both the campaign and his program at age thirty-one or thirty-two. The grand strategy that killed him was partly dictated by the office he held.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: the structural pressure on any eastern emperor; the contingency on which his entire legacy turned
Source: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia_(1913)/Julian_the_Apostate
*Confidence: high. Verify: Death near Samarra 26 June 363 is consistent across retrieved pages (Wikipedia gives Samarra; Greek Wikipedia gives Maranga). Check Ammianus 24-25 for the campaign.*

### C23 — Writing as an emperor's instrument
*Period:* 355-363
Julian wrote in Greek — panegyrics, hymns to Helios and the Mother of the Gods, satires (The Caesars, Misopogon), letters, and Against the Galileans — much of it composed at night amid campaigning and rule, per a Greek popular page. The Wikisource author page confirms the corpus's range. For him writing was not leisure but governance and evangelism: an emperor doing his own apologetics because no pagan hierarchy existed to do it for him.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his need to personally author the religion he was restoring; prose as his second battlefield
Source: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Author:Flavius_Claudius_Iulianus
*Confidence: high. Verify: The work-list is attested by Wikisource; the night-writing habit is from the evprattein.gr popular page — trace before use. Against the Galileans survives only in Cyril's refutation, which I did not retrieve.*

### C24 — No institution to restore, so he invented one
*Period:* 361-363
The Encyclopedia.com entry describes Julian's positive program: a rejuvenated paganism with Neoplatonism as intellectual base and a priesthood modeled on the Christian clergy, with himself as pontifex maximus at its head — and notes it met 'complete apathy.' The circumstance is that paganism had no church, no creed, no clergy ladder; to fight Christianity he had to build a pagan copy of it, and the materials were not there.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: the paradox of his reform: anti-Christian in aim, Christian in form; grounds his letters to high priests like Arsacius
Source: https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/history/ancient-history-late-roman-and-byzantine-biographies/julian-apostate
*Confidence: high. Verify: The program is attested in the entry and in Julian's letters (e.g., to Arsacius, listed in Tougher's contents); the BMCR review notes Tougher under-engages the pagan clergy question — check more recent scholarship.*

**Words: what they wrote or were recorded saying**

### W1 — Reckoning twenty years lost
*Period:* 362
The Wikipedia article on Julian reports that in 362, looking back on his life, Julian wrote that he had spent twenty years in the way of Christianity and twelve in the true way, meaning the way of Helios. This places his private conversion around age twenty, a decade before he could safely avow it. It is his own arithmetic of apostasy, framed as wasted time recovered.
evidence_basis: attested
Bearing on: his self-narrative of conversion and his habit of counting his life in allegiances
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_(emperor)
*Confidence: high. Verify: Check the Wikipedia passage against the underlying Julian text it cites; confirm which 362 work contains the twenty/twelve reckoning.*

### W2 — Parting with Eusebius for Maximus
*Period:* c. 351
Wikipedia, citing the historian Eunapius, reports that when Julian abandoned the sober Eusebius of Myndus for the theurgist Maximus of Ephesus, he told his old teacher to keep to his books, since Eusebius had shown him the man he had been searching for. The page presents Eusebius's warning against conjurers' tricks and Julian's fascination anyway. This is a quotation transmitted through Eunapius, not a document of Julian's.
evidence_basis: attested
Bearing on: his preference for ecstatic theurgy over rationalist philosophy; susceptibility to wonder-workers
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_(emperor)
*Confidence: medium. Verify: Verify against Eunapius's Lives of the Sophists; the wording reached us through two layers of reporting.*

### W3 — Against the Galileans
*Period:* winter 362-363
The 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia on Wikisource states that Julian wrote his books against the Christians while wintering at Antioch in 362-63; Encyclopedia.com's New Catholic Encyclopedia entry says his work Against the Galileans expounds his anti-Christian position. The treatise survives only in fragments quoted by a Christian refuter. His chosen slur for Christians, 'Galileans,' recurs across his letters as reported by the Negri biography on archive.org.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his systematic theological case against Christianity, not mere policy hostility
Source: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia_(1913)/Julian_the_Apostate
*Confidence: high. Verify: Confirm the Antioch dating and the fragmentary transmission via Cyril of Alexandria's refutation.*

### W4 — Misopogon, the beard-hater satire
*Period:* 363
The Edinburgh University Press page for Tougher's sourcebook lists Julian's Misopogon among the key documents, and the Negri biography on archive.org reports its content: Julian's bitter comparison of Antioch's conduct with cities that rebuilt temples, his resentment over the removal of the martyr Babylas's relics from Apollo's temple at Daphne, and his suspicion that Christians burned the temple. Encyclopedia.com calls it a satire offering insight into a noble but erratic character.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his public self-mockery curdled into grievance; the Antioch rupture in his own voice
Source: https://archive.org/stream/julianapostate02negruoft/julianapostate02negruoft_djvu.txt
*Confidence: high. Verify: Check the Misopogon text itself for the Daphne fire passage and the self-caricature of the unkempt emperor.*

### W5 — Rescript on Christian teachers
*Period:* June-August 362
The Christian Scholar's Review article reports that Julian's 17 June 362 edict required teachers be distinguished by character and eloquence, and that his follow-up rescript argued a 'proper education' requires a healthy mind with true opinions, which only worshippers of the gods possess. The Negri biography reports Julian's reasoning in paraphrase: teachers of Homer, Hesiod and Demosthenes who reject the gods those authors honored contradict themselves, and must either teach by example or stop teaching.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his most contested policy argued as consistency, not persecution; coercion through curriculum
Source: https://christianscholars.com/julian-against-christian-educators/
*Confidence: high. Verify: Confirm the edict/rescript sequence and dates against the letter text; Ammianus's 'inhumane' verdict belongs to testimony.*

### W6 — Letter to the citizens of Bostra
*Period:* 362
The Negri biography on archive.org reports Julian's manifesto to Bostra amid threatened pagan-Christian riots: he insists no one commit violence, that the misguided be persuaded by reason rather than blows, and that worshippers of the gods not attack those who err from ignorance. The same page reports his argument that the bishops, not the people, caused the disorders, and describes his handling of Bishop Titus of Bostra as hypocritical enough to stain his character.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his stated toleration alongside manipulation of Christian leaders; the said/did gap in his own correspondence
Source: https://archive.org/stream/julianapostate02negruoft/julianapostate02negruoft_djvu.txt
*Confidence: high. Verify: Check the Letter to the Bostrenses text; confirm the Titus episode and Negri's hostile reading of it.*

### W7 — Hymn to the Mother of the Gods
*Period:* 362
The erenow page from a history of early Byzantium quotes the opening prayer of Julian's Hymn to Cybele: an address to the mother of gods and men as wisdom, providence and creator of souls, asking happiness for all men, knowledge of the gods as the highest happiness, and cleansing of the Roman people from the stain of impiety. The Wikisource author page and Tougher's contents list both this hymn and its pair, To King Helios, among his surviving orations.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his devotional Neoplatonism in his own liturgical voice; piety as his self-image
Source: https://erenow.org/postclassical/the-early-centuries/5.php
*Confidence: medium. Verify: The quoted prayer is a popular-book translation; check against the Loeb text of the Hymn to the Mother of the Gods.*

### W8 — Letter to the Athenians as self-defense
*Period:* 361
The dokumen.pub text of Tougher's study reports that Julian's Letter to the Athenians sketches his Gallic record: the ruined condition of Gaul on his arrival, the recovery of Cologne, Strasbourg as a famous victory, the 358 offensives against the Salii and Chamavi, and his rejection of the prefect Florentius's policy of paying barbarians for Rhine passage. The page notes Julian presents economic recovery as bound up with military success, and that Ammianus largely follows Julian's own account.
evidence_basis: attested
Bearing on: his public justification for usurpation; how he wanted his deeds read
Source: https://dokumen.pub/julian-the-apostate-9781474473286.html
*Confidence: high. Verify: Confirm the letter's date and its relationship to the parallel account in Ammianus book 16-17.*

### W9 — The Caesars, a Saturnalia satire
*Period:* c. 361-362
The Wikisource author page for Flavius Claudius Iulianus lists The Caesars among his satires, alongside the Misopogon, and Tougher's sourcebook contents include it as document 25. The retrieved pages give the genre and title but not the content: a banquet satire judging past emperors. What the text argues about his models, including Marcus Aurelius, is not covered by the retrieved material and is a recorded gap.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: his ranking of imperial predecessors and implicit self-placement; satire as a mode
Source: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Author:Flavius_Claudius_Iulianus
*Confidence: medium. Verify: Existence and genre are attested by the listing pages; content must be checked against the text itself, which was not retrieved.*

It is a court joke about beer versus wine, plausibly from his Gallic years.

### W11 — Panegyrics for Constantius and Eusebia
*Period:* c. 355-360
Tougher's contents list Julian's Panegyric on the Emperor Constantius and Panegyric on the Empress Eusebia, and Wikipedia notes Julian expresses gratitude to Eusebia in his third oration for her intervention saving him in 354. These are formal praises of a cousin he privately blamed for his family's massacre, delivered while he lived under that cousin's power. The pages attest the works; that the praise was coerced dissimulation is my inference from his situation, not stated.
evidence_basis: reconstructed
Bearing on: the decade of concealment; conduct-over-words tension between public praise and private hatred
Source: https://edinburghuniversitypress.com/book-julian-the-apostate.html
*Confidence: high. Verify: Confirm the dating of the two panegyrics and the third oration's thanks to Eusebia.*

### W12 — Letters rebuilding a pagan clergy
*Period:* 362
Tougher's contents list Julian's letters to the high-priest Theodorus, to Arsacius high-priest of Galatia, and a Letter to a Priest.
