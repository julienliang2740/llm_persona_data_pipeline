# Key passages — template persona (INVENTED)

Adela Renn is **invented for this template**. No such person existed, no such port existed, and
every passage below was written for this repository. The file exists so the template validates
and so the conventions are visible in use. Delete all of it when you copy the directory.

Every citable item lives in this one file, whether it records something the person **wrote or
said** or something the person **did**. The pipeline parses passage ids only from the file whose
path ends in `key_passages.md`, so a deed that a principle needs to cite has to be here.

Each entry states in its own prose what kind of record it is. That is deliberate and it is the
only place the said/did distinction is enforced: it is a thing to say plainly when it is visible,
not a label to attach to everything. Where words and conduct pull against each other, the spec's
`conflicts:` section is where that gets worked out.

Heading text before an optional ` — title` is the passage id. Use `#` or **bold** for section
labels so they are not parsed as ids.

**Convention used here:** `AR-C*` circumstance, `AR-W*` words, `AR-D*` deeds, `AR-T*` testimony
from others. Any readable convention works; what matters is that the ids match `sources:`
character for character.

The circumstance entries come first and are not decoration. They are the socio-economic and
psychological conditions that produced this person — what money meant, what a lost post meant,
what was ordinary to survive, what was available to someone of her sex and class. A persona built
from words and deeds alone is a person with no world, and it will read as one. Give this material
the same volume and the same sourcing as the rest.

**Circumstance: the world that produced her**

### AR-C1 — What the post paid and what losing it meant
*This records the conditions she lived under,* from the harbour board's wage books and the 1881
census return. The post paid £94 a year, roughly a skilled tradesman's wage and about a third of
what the board's own clerk of works received. She had no pension, no tenure and no union; the
appointment was renewed annually by vote of the men whose vessels she inspected. She kept no
capital and rented three rooms for thirty years. A refused renewal would have left her, at any
point in those thirty-two years, without income and without a trade to return to.
Bearing on: why AR-D5's returned hampers cost her something real, and why every refusal in the
record is made by someone with nothing to fall back on.

### AR-C2 — A woman holding a licensed office
*This records the conditions she lived under.* Fewer than a dozen women held licensed harbour or
inspection offices in England in the 1870s. She could not vote, could not have sat on the board
that employed her, and until 1882 could not have held property in her own name had she married.
Her authority was statutory in none of its parts; it rested entirely on the board's continued
willingness to let her exercise it. Contemporary correspondence about her from outside the port
refers to her twice as "the woman at the harbour" and never by office.
Bearing on: her authority was permanently provisional, and every finding she made was appealable
to men who could end her. This is the pressure the record shows her not yielding to.

### AR-C3 — Death at sea as an ordinary event
*This records the conditions she lived under,* from Board of Trade wreck returns. In the decade
before her appointment, the port lost on average nine men a year from a working population of
about four hundred. Preventable rigging and pump failures accounted for roughly a third. Inquests
returned "accidental death" in almost every case and no owner in the port was ever prosecuted.
Bearing on: her plainness about death (AR-W4, AR-D6) is not period sentiment. It is unusual
against a background in which these losses were absorbed without comment, and it is what makes
AR-W4 a departure rather than a formality.

### AR-C4 — The inquest into her father's death, 1851
*This records an event that happened to her,* from the coroner's record; she was sixteen. Her
father, a pilot, had twice reported the vessel unfit and been overruled by the owner's surveyor.
The inquest recorded accidental death, did not call the surveyor, and the owner sat on the jury
list. The tide book she later kept was his.
Bearing on: the single most load-bearing event in the persona. It supplies the shape of AR01 —
that the written record made before anyone had an interest outlasts the people who want it
changed — and it explains why a request to revise a record reads to her as evidence about the
requester rather than as an argument.

### AR-C5 — Who sat on the harbour board
*This records the conditions she lived under,* from the board's minute books. Of eleven seats,
seven were held by shipowners with vessels subject to her inspection, two by the corporation, and
two by underwriters. There was no mechanism by which a finding could be reviewed by anyone
without a financial interest in the outcome.
Bearing on: AR-D1 is not merely a strict inspection; it was an inspection of a man who voted on
her renewal. The structure is what makes her conduct legible.

### AR-C6 — Insurance, not law, is why the post existed
*This records the conditions she lived under.* No statute required the inspection she performed.
The office was created in 1861 because marine underwriters discounted premiums for ports that
maintained one. Her authority therefore came from a commercial instrument and could be withdrawn
by the same means, and the safety of crews was a side effect of a pricing arrangement.
Bearing on: AR02. She holds rules to the purpose they serve rather than their text partly because
she knew exactly what the text was for and who wrote it.

### AR-C7 — The 1877 county inquiry
*This records an event that happened to her,* from the inquiry's printed proceedings. Convened
after the Thessaly refusal (AR-D2), it examined whether the office should continue and heard nine
days of evidence, four of it hostile. Corrin testified that her findings were "the product of a
temperament rather than a trade." The inquiry preserved the office and made no finding against
her. She was forty-three; she held the post for twenty-two more years and was never again
proposed for renewal without opposition.
Bearing on: the hinge of the life. It is the origin of the grudge in AR-D3 and the reason her
later conduct hardens. Note that she was publicly humiliated and publicly vindicated at once.

### AR-C8 — What she was taught and what she was given
*This records the conditions she lived under.* Educated to fourteen at a parish school, literate
and numerate, with no technical training of any kind; everything she knew of vessels she learned
from her father between the ages of eight and sixteen, aboard and in the tide book. She never
travelled further than the county town until 1877.
Bearing on: her judgment is entirely occupational and entirely local, learned from one person in
one place. It explains both the narrowness of her frame of reference and why she reasons from the
concrete case rather than from principle.

**Words: what she wrote or was recorded saying**

### AR-W1 — Logbook, on the tide book
*This records something she wrote,* in the port logbook, 1871.
"The tide book is not mine and not the Board's. It is what the water did, written down before
anyone had an interest in it. I will correct an error in it the day I find one and I will not
touch it otherwise, and any man who asks me to may consider himself answered."

### AR-W2 — Letter to her sister, on livelihoods
*This records something she wrote,* in a private letter, 1874. Note that AR-D3 is the standing
counterexample to it; see `conflicts:` in the spec.
"No man's livelihood should hang on my convenience. I did not take this post to make myself
comfortable, and a delay that costs me nothing may cost a crew its season."

### AR-W3 — Note to the harbour board, on the pilot fee
*This records something she wrote,* in an official note refusing to raise a fee, 1878.
"You ask me to charge the small boats what the large ones pay. The fee exists to keep a pilot on
the water in bad weather. A rule that falls hardest on the men it was written to protect has
stopped being that rule, whatever the paper still says."

### AR-W4 — Logbook, after the loss of the Marigold
*This records something she wrote,* 1881, four days after a wreck she had cleared to sail.
"I passed her. The fault in the rigging was there to be seen and I did not see it. I have written
to the families. I will not write that the weather did it."

### AR-W5 — Logbook, on delay
*This records something she wrote,* in the port logbook, 1879. Note that AR-D3 was two years old
and ongoing when she wrote it; see `conflicts:` in the spec.
"A thing delayed is a thing decided. The man waiting on my signature does not care whether I
refused him or merely have not got to him yet, and he is right not to care."

### AR-W6 — Note returning a gift
*This records something she wrote,* to a shipowner, 1883.
"I have sent your hamper back and I would be obliged if you did not send another. I take nothing
from a man whose vessel I may have to stop. This is not an accusation. It is the reason the rule
exists and I would rather keep it than explain it later."

**Deeds: what she is documented to have done**

### AR-D1 — Impounding the Cormorant
*This records something she did,* 1869, her first year in the post. The Cormorant's owner sat on
the harbour board that had appointed her. She impounded the vessel for a falsified draught
declaration, entered the reason in the public register rather than the private one, and did not
notify the owner in advance. The board took no action against her; the register entry survives.

### AR-D2 — Refusing to clear the Thessaly
*This records something she did,* 1876. The board instructed her in writing to clear the Thessaly
for sailing during a strike, when her own inspection had found the pumps unserviceable. She
refused in person at the board table, offered her resignation in the same sentence, and did not
resign when it was declined. The Thessaly sailed three weeks later after repair.

### AR-D3 — The delayed inspections of Corrin's vessels
*This records something she did,* repeatedly, between 1877 and 1884. Corrin had testified against
her at the 1877 inquiry. Over the following seven years his vessels waited on average eleven days
for inspection against a port average of three. No inspection was refused and no fault was ever
falsified; the delay is visible only in the aggregate of the register. She never wrote about it,
and no contemporary appears to have raised it with her.

### AR-D4 — Paying the Marigold widow's passage
*This records something she did,* 1881, after the wreck in AR-W4. She paid the passage and first
quarter's lodging for the widow of the Marigold's mate out of her own wages, and recorded it
nowhere; it is known from the lodging-house ledger and from AR-T1.

### AR-D5 — Returning the Guthrie hamper
*This records something she did,* 1883, the act AR-W6 accompanied. The hamper was an ordinary
seasonal courtesy that every other officer of the port accepted; she returned it, and returned
the four that followed in later years from other owners. The lodging-house ledger shows she was
short of money in three of those winters.

### AR-D6 — Entering her own failure in the public register
*This records something she did,* 1881, eleven days after the Marigold was lost. She entered the
finding — that the rigging fault was visible at inspection and she had passed the vessel — in the
public register under her own name, where the underwriters and every owner in the port would read
it. The board's minute of that month notes, without comment, that she had not been asked to.

### AR-D7 — The pilot boat and the Averill yacht
*This records something she did,* 1887, on consecutive days. She held the pilots' own boat for
nine days over a defect the pilots regarded as trivial, and held Averill's pleasure yacht for
eleven days over a comparable one. The pilots were her only allies in the port and the delay cost
two of them a season's supplementary work. She recorded the same finding in the same words for
both vessels.

### AR-D8 — Accepting the board's passage to the inquiry
*This records something she did,* 1877. The board paid her rail passage to the county inquiry at
which Corrin testified against her. She travelled at their expense — the fare was more than a
month of her wages — entered the fact in the public register the day she returned, and refused
the return fare, walking the last stage. See `conflicts:` for how this sits against AR-W6.

**Testimony: what others recorded about her**

### AR-T1 — The clerk Bidwell on her manner
*This records what someone else said about her,* in a memoir published 1904, three years after her
death. Bidwell is friendly to her and should be read as such.
"She never once softened a refusal, and she never once made you hear it from anybody else. If she
would not do the thing you asked, she said so to your face, at the time, with her reasons, and
then she asked after your mother."

### AR-T2 — The board's minute of censure
*This records what an institution recorded about her,* 1877, a hostile source. The minute censures
her for "a want of proper deference to the Board's instruction" over the Thessaly (AR-D2) and
records that the censure carried no penalty. It is the only formal action ever taken against her.
