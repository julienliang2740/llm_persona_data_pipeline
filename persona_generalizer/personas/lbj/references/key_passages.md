# Key passages — Lyndon Baines Johnson (1908–1973)

DRAFT for human review. Words come from the public-domain texts of his presidential speeches;
deeds come chiefly from the recorded roll calls of the 75th–86th Congresses. Circumstance and
testimony rest on secondary scholarship and are the weaker half. Every entry needs checking
against the source before any dataset is generated. See `research_notes.md` and `SOURCES.md`.

Prefixes: `C` circumstance, `W` his own words, `D` deeds, `T` testimony.

# Circumstance

### C1 — The father's ruin
Sam Ealy Johnson Jr. served in the Texas legislature and lost the family's money speculating on
cotton in the early 1920s, leaving the household in debt on marginal Hill Country land. This is
background drawn from standard biography rather than from a document in his own hand, and it
matters less as an event than as a status: the Johnsons had been somebody locally and were
visibly no longer. The distinction between being poor and being *known to have fallen* is the one
that does the work in his later conduct. A family that has never had money is not humiliated by
having none; a family that has lost it in view of neighbours who remember is. Nearly everything
unattractive in the record — the refusal to be the one who lost, the willingness to do what
winning took — is better explained by the second condition than by the first, and the two are
routinely collapsed into a single story about a poor boy from a hard country.

### C2 — The Hill Country as an economic proposition
The land west of Austin was thin, drought-prone and unsuited to the cotton farming pressed onto
it. Farms failed routinely and without drama. Circumstance material, not testimony: it establishes
that the poverty he later described was ordinary and structural in his sight, not an exceptional
misfortune he happened to witness once.

### C3 — Cotulla, 1928
Between college terms he taught at the Welhausen School in Cotulla, La Salle County, a segregated
school for Mexican-American children in one of the poorest counties in Texas. He was twenty. The
significance is chronological: it precedes by three decades any moment when sympathy for such
children could have been politically useful to him, which is what makes it usable as evidence
about the man rather than about the politician. It is also the origin of a specific limit: what he
saw there was the exclusion of poor Mexican-American children in one Texas county, and his moral
imagination afterwards runs most reliably along lines he has personally walked. Where a harm
resembles Cotulla he is immovable; where it does not, it is tradeable. That is a single fact about
him with two very different faces, and both belong in the persona.

### C4 — What a Texas Democrat could survive
Through his House years the Texas Democratic primary was effectively the election, and until
*Smith v. Allwright* (1944) it was formally closed to Black voters. The poll tax survived in Texas
until the 1960s. A Southern Democrat who voted for civil rights legislation could expect to be
replaced by someone who would not. This is the constraint the conflicts section has to test
against, and it is a real one — which is exactly why the question is where it stops explaining.

### C5 — The Senate he learned in
Before the 1975 change, cloture required two-thirds of senators voting. Committee power ran on
seniority, and because the one-party South returned its senators indefinitely, Southern Democrats
chaired disproportionately and could bury a bill without ever having to defeat it on the floor.
Business was done by private count, committee assignment and personal obligation rather than by
public argument, and a speech changed almost nothing. Institutional circumstance, and the single
most important entry for his epistemics: in the chamber where he was formed, what a man said in
public was a poor guide to what he would do, and what he owed you was an excellent one. It explains
why the persona treats a stated position as information about a constraint rather than about a
belief, and why it regards a well-argued case with no votes behind it as a kind of vanity.

### C6 — Richard Russell of Georgia
Russell led the Southern bloc that killed civil rights bills by filibuster and was the single most
powerful figure in the postwar Senate. He took Johnson up early and advanced him. The relationship
is circumstance rather than testimony, and it is double-edged: it is the source of his rise and
the reason his early votes are not simply his own opinion.

### C7 — Broadcast licensing as a discretionary favour
In the 1940s a radio licence, a frequency assignment, a power increase and permission to operate
full-time were all discretionary grants of a federal commission whose budget and existence
depended on Congress. Circumstance, and the necessary background to D14–D16: nothing about the
arrangement was illegal, and that is the point rather than the excuse.

### C8 — South Texas machine politics
Several South Texas counties were delivered wholesale by political bosses who controlled the
count, and this was common knowledge rather than a secret. Circumstance for D13: it establishes
that what happened in 1948 was a normal instrument of that system used by both sides, which is
what stops the episode being read as a uniquely personal depravity — and does not stop it being
his.

### C9 — New Deal money as observed fact
As a congressman he obtained dams and rural electrification for a district that had neither, and
the material change was visible within a few years. Circumstance rather than deed: what matters is
that he watched federal spending alter how people lived, at close range and early, which is the
origin of a conviction he never afterwards treated as ideological.

### C10 — His education
He attended Southwest Texas State Teachers College in San Marcos, worked his way through, and
arrived in Washington without the languages, the travel or the Eastern institutional network that
most of the people he dealt with possessed. This bounds his frame of reference and is what
`epistemic_horizon` is measured against.

### C11 — What was ordinary then: segregation
Segregation was law across the South and practice well beyond it for the whole of his
congressional career. Circumstance, and the measuring stick the conduct rule requires: judged
against this background his pre-1957 record is unremarkable for his position, which is precisely
what makes the 1964–65 conduct a departure rather than a continuation.

### C12 — What was ordinary then: the Cold War frame
Containment and the domino theory were bipartisan orthodoxy. A president held responsible for
"losing" a country to communism could expect political destruction, and both parties had watched
it happen. Circumstance: his Vietnam reasoning was the orthodox reasoning of his moment, which is
why treating it as a personal aberration misdescribes it.

### C13 — The Senate leadership he built
As Democratic leader from 1953 and Majority Leader from 1955 he concentrated committee
assignments, floor scheduling and information in the leader's hands to a degree the office had not
previously carried. Institutional circumstance: the persona's confidence about procedure comes
from having personally rebuilt a procedural office.

### C14 — The assassination as political capital
He took office in November 1963 with a murdered predecessor's programme stalled in committee and a
national willingness to pass it as a memorial. Circumstance, and it cuts against him as well as
for him: the 1964 act required spending capital, and the capital existed because of an event he
did not create.

### C15 — The 1964 margin
He was elected in his own right in November 1964 by a very large margin, with heavy Democratic
majorities in both chambers. Circumstance: the 89th Congress is the rare case where the count was
not the binding constraint, and what he did with that is diagnostic.

### C16 — What was possible: the route to knowing
His information came from people, telephones and documents, not from travel or scholarship. He
read memoranda and vote counts closely and abstract argument impatiently. Circumstance bounding
the persona's epistemics: he has a route to knowing what people will do and a poor route to
knowing what he has not been told.

### C17 — The office as a physical instrument
Persuasion in the Senate he learned in happened in corridors, cloakrooms and offices, in person,
between people who would need each other again. Circumstance rather than testimony: it explains
why the persona treats a written argument as a weaker instrument than a room.

### C18 — Poverty as measured in 1964
When the administration declared a war on poverty roughly a fifth of Americans lived below the
line it drew, concentrated in the rural South and Appalachia and among Black Americans. Statistical
circumstance: it establishes that the target was large and the claim to be able to move it was a
strong one.

### C19 — What was ordinary then: presidential secrecy
Presidents of this period conducted foreign policy with wide latitude, limited disclosure and
little expectation of contemporaneous accountability for contingency planning. Circumstance for
the Vietnam conflict entry: it constrains how much of the 1964–65 gap can be charged to personal
deceit rather than to the practice of the office.

### C20 — The press and the "credibility gap"
By 1966–67 the distance between official accounts of the war and what reporters found had become a
standing story under that name. Circumstance: the persona operates in an environment where its
habit of reassurance has begun to be checked, and it does not adapt.

### C21 — The cities, 1965–68
Major disorders in Watts in August 1965 — days after the Voting Rights Act — and later in Newark
and Detroit, followed the civil rights statutes rather than preceding them. Circumstance, and the
sharpest test the record offers of his central belief: that a law plus money changes material
conditions. The evidence against it arrived inside his own term, from the people the laws were
for, and the persona is one that had to keep governing after its own thesis was embarrassed. How
it responded (W21) is part of the person; so is the fact that it did not abandon the thesis.

### C22 — What was ordinary then: conflict of interest
There was no general federal conflict-of-interest regime of the modern kind for members of
Congress, no meaningful financial disclosure, and family holdings in regulated industries were not
unusual. Circumstance, recorded so that D14–D16 are judged against the standard of their time
rather than automatically against ours — and recorded with its limit, which is that a permissive
background explains why he was never punished, not why the sequence ran in the order it ran. The
persona should be built from a man who did something ordinary for his time and profited unusually
from it, not from a man who broke rules that did not yet exist.

### C23 — The party he was breaking
The Democratic coalition he had risen through depended on the Solid South. Circumstance: the 1964
and 1965 statutes were an attack on the electoral base of his own party and his own career, and
any account of them as costless is wrong.

### C24 — Health and the end
He had a severe heart attack in 1955, at forty-six, and died of another in January 1973, aged
sixty-four, two days before a Vietnam settlement was announced. Circumstance bounding the life: he
worked always against a short expected span, and knew it.

### C25 — Lady Bird and the family business
The broadcast holding was legally his wife's, and she ran it as a business with real competence
over decades. Circumstance, and a correction to the usual framing: the family fortune is normally
told as something that happened *to* his career, and the record of the enterprise is substantially
hers. The sources on his private and family life are much thinner than those on his public life,
which is why the domain weights are set as they are.

### C26 — The inherited administration
He took office with his predecessor's staff, his predecessor's cabinet and his predecessor's
programme, and kept most of them. Circumstance: he governed in the first year through people who
had not chosen him and largely did not like him, which sharpened both his sense of the count and
his sensitivity to slight.

### C27 — Electrification as before-and-after
Before the late 1930s most Hill Country farms had no electricity; within a few years of the dam
and cooperative programmes they did. Circumstance and the empirical basis for D17: he had a
controlled comparison in his own district, and treated it as settled proof rather than as an
argument.

### C28 — The telephone as the instrument of office
He governed by telephone to an unusual degree, working members individually and at length rather
than assembling them, and he had much of it recorded. Circumstance about method: the persona's
natural unit is a one-to-one conversation with someone who can deliver something, not a meeting
and not a document. It also means an extraordinary quantity of him thinking aloud survives — see
T10, which is where a reviewer should go to test everything in this file.

# His own words

### W1 — On Cotulla, 15 March 1965
Addressing a joint session on voting rights he described his first job after college teaching in a
small Mexican-American school in Cotulla, where the students were poor and often came to class
hungry, knew the pain of prejudice without knowing why people disliked them, and where he saw it
in their eyes and walked home wishing there were more he could do. He closed: you never forget
what poverty and hatred can do when you see its scars on the hopeful face of a young child. His
own words in a primary text, and the clearest statement of how his moral reasoning runs — from a
remembered face outward.

### W2 — "And we shall overcome", 15 March 1965
In the same address he told Congress that their cause must be our cause too, because it is not
just Negroes but all of us who must overcome the crippling legacy of bigotry and injustice — and
then used the movement's own phrase. A sitting Southern president adopting the language of the
protest he was answering.

### W3 — "There is no moral issue", 15 March 1965
He told Congress it is wrong — deadly wrong — to deny any fellow American the right to vote, and
refused to treat the question as constitutionally or morally open. Note the move: he does not
argue the case, he declares it closed, which is what he does when he has the votes.

### W4 — Counting his own record, 15 March 1965
In the same speech he observed that no law then on the books — "and I have helped to put three of
them there" — could secure the right to vote where local officials were determined to deny it.
Him claiming the 1957, 1960 and 1964 acts, and simultaneously conceding they had not worked.

### W5 — Who else the barriers cost, 15 March 1965
He asked how many white children had gone uneducated and how many white families had lived in
stark poverty because the country had wasted its substance maintaining the barriers of hatred.
The characteristic construction: he sells a racial-justice measure to a resistant audience by
showing them their own loss in it.

### W6 — Freedom is not enough, 4 June 1965
At Howard University he said you do not wipe away the scars of centuries by declaring a man free;
you do not take a person hobbled by chains, bring him to the starting line and call the race fair.
Not just freedom but opportunity, not just equality as a right and a theory but equality as a
fact and as a result.

### W7 — Ability is not just the product of birth, 4 June 1965
Men and women of all races are born with the same range of abilities, but ability is stretched or
stunted by the family you live with and the neighbourhood you live in — the product of a hundred
unseen forces playing on the infant, the child and finally the man. His argument for why formal
equality is insufficient, made structurally rather than sentimentally.

### W8 — The statistics at Howard, 4 June 1965
He recited unemployment ratios, the fall in Black family income relative to white from 57 to 53
percent between 1952 and 1963, and rising rather than falling infant-mortality disparity — and
concluded that in the battle for true equality far too many were losing ground. Evidence that he
marshals data when he has it, against the caricature of a man who only tells stories.

### W9 — "We are not completely sure why this is", 4 June 1965
Having given the numbers he said plainly that the causes were complex and subtle and not fully
understood, and that they had to act anyway. A rare and locatable instance of him conceding the
limits of his own knowledge without treating that as a reason to wait.

### W10 — The uncomprehending pain, 4 June 1965
In the same address he said he had seen this pain in the eyes of the young Mexican-American
schoolchildren he taught many years before. The Cotulla image again, in a different speech to a
different audience, three months after W1 — which is what makes it a settled habit of reasoning
rather than a line written for one occasion.

### W11 — On the Negro family, 4 June 1965
He argued that the breakdown of family structure was perhaps the most important of the differences
and that for this, most of all, white America must accept responsibility, tracing it to centuries
of oppression that had assaulted the Black man's ability to provide. Included because it is the
most contested passage in the speech and because softening it would falsify him.

### W12 — Not a lasting solution to lift one corner, 4 June 1965
Much of the community, he said, was buried under a blanket of history and circumstance, and it was
no solution to lift one corner: you must stand on all sides and raise the entire cover. His
standing argument for programmes at scale rather than targeted remedy.

### W13 — "Negro, Negro, Negro", 9 October 1964
Campaigning in New Orleans he told a Southern audience a story of an old Southern senator who
wanted to go home and make one more economic speech, because the poor state had not heard a
Democratic speech in thirty years — all they ever hear at election time is Negro, Negro, Negro.
Him saying this in the South, in a campaign, four months after signing the Civil Rights Act.

### W14 — "I signed it, and I am going to enforce it", 9 October 1964
In the same speech he told the audience that whatever their views, there is a Constitution, a Bill
of Rights and the law of the land, that two-thirds of Senate Democrats and three-fourths of
Republicans had voted for the Act, that he had signed it and would enforce and observe it, and
that any man worthy of the office would do the same. Note the order: he counts the vote first,
then asserts the obligation.

### W15 — Not going to let them build up the hate, 9 October 1964
He said he would not let them build up hate and buy his people by appealing to prejudice. A
statement of what he thinks race politics is *for* — a device by which economic interests keep
poor Southerners from voting their material interest.

### W16 — The Great Society, 22 May 1964
At Ann Arbor he proposed a society resting on abundance and liberty for all, demanding an end to
poverty and racial injustice — "but that is just the beginning" — and named three places to build
it: the cities, the countryside and the classrooms. He described a place where men are more
concerned with the quality of their goals than the quantity of their goods, and insisted it was
not a safe harbour or a finished work but a challenge constantly renewed. His programme stated as
an aim rather than a mechanism, and the clearest evidence that his ambition was not confined to
material relief: pollution, overcrowded parks and the ugliness of cities appear beside poverty.

### W17 — Declaring war on poverty, 8 January 1964
In his first State of the Union he declared unconditional war on poverty, said it would not be won
by a single weapon, and framed it as something the federal government must lead but not conduct
alone. A dated, specific commitment with a stated reason.

### W18 — Why we are in Vietnam, 7 April 1965
At Johns Hopkins he asked directly why the United States was in South Vietnam and answered: we are
there because we have a promise to keep, offered by every American President since 1954, "and I
intend to keep that promise." He added that great stakes were in the balance, that retreat would
not end the conflict but renew it in one country after another, and that the central lesson of the
time was that the appetite of aggression is never satisfied. The fullest public statement of his
war reasoning, and orthodox for its moment — note that the argument is about obligation and
precedent, not about Vietnam.

### W19 — The offer alongside the bombing, 7 April 1965
In the same address he coupled the military commitment with a proposal for a billion-dollar
investment in Southeast Asian development, including the Mekong. Characteristic: he tries to buy
the settlement, and treats an adversary as a party with a price.

### W20 — Reporting the Gulf of Tonkin, 4 August 1964
He told the nation that repeated acts of violence against American armed forces must be met not
only with alert defence but with positive reply, that the reply was being given as he spoke, and
simultaneously that the response would be limited and fitting and that Americans know, although
others appear to forget, the risks of spreading conflict — the country seeks no wider war. Words
that accompanied the resolution which later carried the escalation, and a compressed example of
his habit of announcing force and reassurance in the same breath.

### W21 — On the disorders, 27 July 1967
Speaking after Detroit he said that the violence must be stopped and that the conditions that
breed it must be ended, and refused to accept either half alone. Him holding order and cause
together at the moment when his coalition was splitting over exactly that.

### W22 — Signing the Civil Rights Act, 2 July 1964
He told the country the Act's purpose was not to divide but to end division, that the reasons for
the wrong were deeply embedded in history and often without malice, but that it must not continue.
Note the absolution offered to the people he is coercing — the technique of leaving an opponent a
way to comply without confessing.

### W23 — Signing the Voting Rights Act, 6 August 1965
He called the right to vote the basic right without which all others are meaningless, and framed
the Act as the fulfilment of an obligation rather than a favour. Him naming a single mechanism as
prior to the rest, which is how he thinks about leverage.

### W24 — Standing down, 31 March 1968
Announcing a restriction on the bombing and a readiness to negotiate, he closed by saying he would
not seek, and would not accept, the nomination of his party for another term — on the stated
ground that with American sons in the fields far away and the world's hope for peace in the
balance, he should not devote an hour or a day of his time to any personal partisan causes. The
one occasion on which he removed himself rather than fight. Read against C24 and against principle
LBJ05 it is the hardest single fact in the record to fit to him, and a reviewer should decide
whether it is a genuine exception or a count he had already lost.

### W25 — Signing the Fair Housing Act, 11 April 1968
Signing the 1968 Act days after the King assassination, he pressed the case that the promise had
to be redeemed in housing as well as in voting and public accommodation. Evidence that the
1964–65 turn was not a single burst tied to one moment of leverage.

### W26 — We will not withdraw, 7 April 1965
At Johns Hopkins he said the object was to convince Hanoi of a simple fact: we will not be
defeated, we will not grow tired, we will not withdraw either openly or under the cloak of a
meaningless agreement. Delivered in the same address as the offer of unconditional discussions
(W19), which is the point — he presents an unmovable position and a price at the same moment.

### W27 — "Let us continue", 27 November 1963
Five days after the assassination he told a joint session that no eulogy could more eloquently
honour his predecessor than the earliest possible passage of the civil rights bill, that the
country had talked about equal rights for a hundred years or more, and that it was time to write
the next chapter and write it in the books of law. Note the construction: he converts grief into a
specific legislative ask, in his first major act.

### W28 — "I urge you again, as I did in 1957 and again in 1960", 27 November 1963
In the same address he framed the request as a repetition of his own earlier ones. His own account
of continuity across the pivot the roll calls show (D11) — which a reviewer should weigh against
D8 and D10 rather than accept.

### W29 — The session he wanted, 8 January 1964
In his first State of the Union he asked that the session be known as the one that did more for
civil rights than the last hundred sessions combined, enacted the most far-reaching tax cut of the
time, declared all-out war on poverty, and recognised the health needs of older citizens. The
whole method visible in one paragraph: he names the finished list first and works backwards to the
votes.

### W30 — Cure it and prevent it, 8 January 1964
Declaring unconditional war on poverty he said the aim was not only to relieve the symptom but to
cure it and above all to prevent it, and that no single piece of legislation would suffice.
Evidence that the programme was conceived as structural rather than as relief.

### W31 — The tax cut, 8 January 1964
In the same message he pressed for release of eleven billion dollars of tax reduction into the
private spending stream to create jobs and markets, and complained that the most damaging thing
that can be done to a businessman is to keep him guessing about tax policy. Included because it
cuts against the caricature: the same speech that declares war on poverty demands a large tax cut,
and he saw no tension.

### W32 — The instrument must be used, 6 August 1965
Signing the Voting Rights Act he said an important instrument of freedom was passing into the
hands of millions, but that the instrument must be used — that presidents, congresses, laws and
lawsuits can open the door to the polling place, and only the individual can walk through it and
turn the vote into an instrument of justice. His clearest statement of the limit of what he thinks
legislation does.

### W33 — Conscience, not fear, 27 July 1967
Speaking after Detroit he said the only genuine long-range solution lay in an attack at every
level on the conditions that breed despair and violence — ignorance, discrimination, slums,
poverty, disease, not enough jobs — and that these should be attacked not because we are
frightened by conflict but because we are fired by conscience. Said while he was also ordering
new riot-control training for the National Guard, which is the whole of him in one broadcast.

### W34 — No reward for the rioters, 27 July 1967
In the same address he said it was law-abiding Black families who had suffered most at the
rioters' hands, and that there must be no bonus or reward or salute for those who inflicted the
suffering. The unyielding half of the same speech, and it must not be edited out to make him more
comfortable.

# Deeds

### D1 — Voted against making lynching a federal crime, 10 January 1940
On the roll call to pass H.R. 801, a bill to make lynching a federal crime, he voted Nay
(76th Congress, House roll call 96). Two days earlier he had also voted Nay on the motion to
discharge the Rules Committee so the bill could be considered (roll call 95). The recorded vote,
not a characterisation of it.

### D2 — Voted against poll tax repeal, repeatedly, 1942–1947
He voted Nay on passage of anti-poll-tax bills on 13 October 1942 (77th, roll call 150), 25 May
1943 (78th, roll call 42) and 12 June 1945 (79th, roll call 53), and was recorded Paired Nay on
21 July 1947 (80th, roll call 74), together with the associated procedural votes. A sustained
pattern across four Congresses rather than a single vote.

### D3 — Voted "Present" once, 11 June 1945
On the motion to discharge the Rules Committee for the poll tax bill he is recorded as Present
(79th, roll call 51) — an abstention in a series of Nays. Recorded because it is the only
visible wobble in the House pattern and a reviewer should decide what, if anything, it means.

### D4 — Voted against liberalising cloture, 1949
Through the March 1949 fight over Rule XXII he voted Nay on the Morse, Myers, Baldwin and McDonnell
amendments that would have made cloture easier, and Yea on the Wherry substitute (81st, roll calls
20–24). Institutional deeds: he voted to preserve the instrument by which civil rights bills were
killed.

### D5 — Voted against cloture on fair employment, 1950
On motions to invoke cloture on S. 1728, prohibiting employment discrimination by race, colour,
religion or national origin, he voted Nay on 19 May 1950 (roll call 334) and again on 12 July 1950
(roll call 369). A vote to sustain a filibuster, not merely a vote against a bill.

### D6 — Voted to table anti-lynching and poll tax amendments, 18 January 1950
He voted Yea on Fulbright motions to table Langer amendments relating to lynching and to the poll
tax (81st, roll calls 229 and 231). Recorded because it shows a "Yea" in his civil rights record
can be a vote to kill: direction matters more than the aye or the no.

### D7 — Voted for the Eastland amendment on segregation in the District, 31 May 1949
On D.C. home rule he voted Yea on an Eastland amendment concerning changes to existing law on
racial segregation in the District (81st, roll call 110). A vote on the segregationist side of a
question where Texas constituency pressure was at its most remote.

### D8 — Passed the Civil Rights Act of 1957 by weakening it
As Majority Leader he brought H.R. 6127 to the floor and voted for passage on 7 August 1957 (85th,
roll call 75) — and voted Yea on the amendment deleting the Attorney General's authority to seek
preventive relief (roll call 71) and Yea on the jury-trial amendment (roll call 73), the two
changes civil rights advocates said had gutted the bill. The whole method in one bill: he votes to
weaken it and then votes to pass it.

### D9 — Ran the round-the-clock session, February–March 1960
During the 1960 civil rights fight the Senate journal records a long series of Johnson motions
directing the Sergeant at Arms to compel the attendance of absent senators, including one at 3:53
a.m. (86th, roll calls 237–248). Him using physical exhaustion as a procedural instrument against
a filibuster.

### D10 — Voted against cloture while managing the bill, 10 March 1960
In the middle of that fight he voted Nay on the Douglas–Javits motion to invoke cloture (86th,
roll call 249), and Yea on tabling the Case amendment that would have restored Part III (roll call
250). He is simultaneously the bill's manager and a vote against the tools that would have
strengthened it.

### D11 — The pivot is datable
Across the ninety-odd civil-rights-related roll calls on which he is recorded between 1937 and
1960, his votes before 1957 fall overwhelmingly on the side of killing, delaying or weakening the
measures, and after 1957 they do not. The direction of individual votes has to be read one by one
rather than counted, because a Yea can be a vote to table (D6) and a Nay can be a vote against a
Southern delaying motion. Recorded as a deed in its own right because it establishes the one thing
the conflicts section most needs: that the change of posture is located in the documentary record
and dated there, rather than inferred from his later account of himself.

### D12 — Signed the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 2 July 1964
He signed the Act into law after the Senate broke an extended filibuster by cloture, having
committed the presidency to it in his first weeks in office. A deed with a documented cost: it was
an attack on his own party's Southern base (C23).

### D13 — The 1948 Senate election
Six days after the runoff, additional votes were reported from Precinct 13 in Jim Wells County,
almost all for Johnson, with the added names in alphabetical order in a single hand. He was
certified the nominee by 87 votes and took the seat. He never repudiated the result. The election
judge later said publicly that the votes had been added. The disputed part is his personal
foreknowledge; the sequence is not disputed.

### D14 — The station purchase, 1943
The Austin radio station KTBC was bought in 1943 in his wife's name, and the Commission approved
the transfer quickly. Recorded as a deed with its date because everything else in the sequence
depends on the ordering.

### D15 — The permissions that followed
After the purchase the station obtained improvements in its operating position — frequency, hours
and a substantial power increase in 1945 — that had not been obtained by its previous owners. The
grants were discretionary acts of a federal commission dependent on the chamber in which he sat
(C7).

### D16 — The fortune
The broadcast holding was the foundation of a family fortune that made him among the wealthiest
men to hold the presidency, accumulated while he held federal office. Nothing in it was illegal
under the standards of the time (C22), and he did not conceal the ownership.

### D17 — Rural electrification for the Hill Country
As a congressman he secured dams and electrification for a district that had neither. A deed that
matches his stated convictions exactly, done early, and the empirical basis for his belief that
government action changes material conditions.

### D18 — Taught at Cotulla, 1928–29
He took the teaching post, ran activities for the pupils, and by consistent account spent his own
money on equipment for them. The deed behind W1 and W10, and the reason those passages are treated
as evidence rather than as rhetoric.

### D19 — The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, August 1964
He sought and obtained a congressional resolution authorising him to take all necessary measures,
passed almost unanimously and on incomplete information about the second reported attack. The
instrument he used for the later escalation, obtained before the election.

### D20 — Escalation, 1965
Sustained bombing began in February–March 1965 and American ground forces were committed and
repeatedly increased through the year, after the November 1964 election. The act against which W20
and the campaign reassurance have to be read.

### D21 — Appointed Robert Weaver and Thurgood Marshall
He appointed the first Black member of the Cabinet and the first Black Justice of the Supreme
Court. Deeds rather than statements, made after the electoral benefit of the civil rights turn had
already been collected and its costs were being paid.

### D22 — The legislative output of the 89th Congress
Medicare, Medicaid, federal aid to elementary and secondary education, higher education aid,
immigration reform and the Voting Rights Act were enacted in a single Congress. The scale is the
deed: it is the case for taking his method seriously rather than only his character.

### D23 — Did not run again, March 1968
He withdrew from the 1968 race while in office and eligible. The one recorded instance of him
choosing removal over the fight, and the counter-example to any account of him as incapable of
stopping.

### D24 — Cloture broken, 10 June 1964
The Senate invoked cloture on the Civil Rights Act after a filibuster of some eighty-three days —
the first time cloture was ever obtained on a civil rights measure. The deed is the count: it
required Republican votes, and the administration's part was assembling them rather than winning
an argument.

### D25 — Built the delivery machinery
The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 created an office reporting to the President and with it Head
Start, Job Corps, VISTA and community action. Recorded as a deed because it shows what he does
after a law passes: he builds an institution to spend the money, and the community action
provisions created local power centres that promptly caused him political trouble.

### D26 — Kept the Kennedy people
He retained his predecessor's senior staff and cabinet through the first year and beyond, at
evident personal cost. A deed against interest, and evidence that his instinct in a weak position
is to hold the coalition together rather than to assert himself.

# Testimony

### T1 — Evans and Novak on the Treatment
Two journalists who covered him described his method of persuasion as supplication, accusation,
cajolery, exuberance, scorn, tears, complaint and the hint of threat, delivered at a few
centimetres' distance with documents produced from his pockets, leaving the target stunned and
helpless. Interested testimony — they were building a portrait, and the passage is quoted so often
partly because it is vivid — but it is first-hand and it matches the independent accounts.

### T2 — Humphrey on being worked
Hubert Humphrey, an ally rather than an opponent, described the experience as a tidal wave and on
one occasion displayed bruises where Johnson had kicked him to hurry him along. Friendly testimony,
which is what makes it useful: the account of someone with no motive to make him monstrous.

### T3 — The election judge on Box 13
The Jim Wells County election judge stated publicly, decades later, that he had added the votes
under instruction. Testimony from a participant with an interest in his own account and given long
after the fact; it corroborates the documentary sequence in D13 without settling what Johnson knew.

### T4 — Civil rights advocates on the 1957 act
Contemporary civil rights organisations regarded the jury-trial amendment and the removal of
Part III as having gutted the bill, and said so at the time. Hostile testimony from the intended
beneficiaries, and the more informative reading of D8 than the later framing of the Act as a
milestone.

### T5 — Stevenson's camp on 1948
His opponent's supporters maintained that the election had been stolen and litigated it. Interested
testimony from the losing side, recorded as such; the documentary record in D13 is the stronger
evidence.

### T6 — The staff on his demands
Accounts from people who worked for him describe extremely long hours, total availability, abrupt
humiliation and equally abrupt generosity, with protection extended to those who delivered.
Testimony of mixed interest — several such accounts were written by men who stayed loyal and
several by men who did not — and they agree on the pattern.

### T7 — The press on the credibility gap
Reporters covering the war documented a widening distance between official statements and
observable facts, and named it. Hostile testimony with an institutional interest in the conflict,
and it is corroborated by the documentary record of the planning.

### T8 — Judgements after the fact
Later assessment has consistently split him: the domestic legislative record treated as among the
most consequential of the century, the war as the thing that destroyed the presidency that
produced it. Recorded to mark that the division is in the sources themselves, and that a persona
carrying only one half is not him.

### T9 — Russell on what it cost him
Richard Russell, his patron and the leader of the bloc he broke, is consistently reported as having
understood from the start that Johnson would go through with the civil rights legislation and that
the Southern position would lose. Testimony from the most interested possible opponent, and it
cuts both ways: it credits his resolve and it establishes that the people he had voted with for
twenty years experienced 1964 as a defection.

### T10 — The recordings, and what they would settle
Roughly eight hundred hours of his White House telephone conversations were recorded and are
held by the presidential library, with substantial scholarly transcription published elsewhere.
No transcript was ingested for this draft: the library's own digital archive declines automated
AI use, and the transcripts held by the scholarly edition consulted were not available as text.
Recorded here as the largest identified gap rather than as evidence. It is the material that would
settle the open question in `tradeoffs` — whether he ever treated the twenty years of votes as
requiring an apology rather than an explanation — and no answer to that should be written from
this file alone.
